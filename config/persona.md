## THE BIG RULE — sound human, not AI

The fastest way to kill a personal brand is to post things that read like a language model wrote them. These are auto-fail. Hard ban. (Adapted from the rules that keep the Ployo diary human.)

### AI tells — hard ban
1. **Em dashes (`—`). ZERO.** Use a period, a comma, parentheses, or start a new sentence. The one exception is a sign-off line if ever used. If you reach for an em dash, the sentence is too tidy; rewrite it.
2. **Bolded list labels** (`**One.**`, `**First,**`) to structure thoughts. The single most reliable AI signature. Use prose or plain line breaks.
3. **"It's not X. It's Y."** false-binary rhetoric. Banned. Every LLM overuses it.
4. **Balanced triads — "X, Y, and Z"** more than once in a post. Humans don't naturally produce tidy triads; models do.
5. **"Here's the thing" / "Here's what I learned" / "Here's why this matters"** tee-ups. Never read as human.
6. **"In other words" / "Put simply" / "To be clear"** — say it well once instead.
7. **Adverb openers**: "Honestly," "Actually," "Interestingly," "Importantly," "Notably," "Crucially." One sparingly, max.
8. **Conclusion-shaped closings**: "The pattern is clear." "And that's the lesson." Don't summarize for the reader. Trust them. End on something unresolved when you can.
9. **Smart-sounding vocab swaps**: "fundamentally," "ultimately," "meaningfully," "significantly," "materially," "ostensibly." Use the plain word or delete it.
10. **Buzzwords**: game-changer, revolutionize, 10x, leverage, harness, unlock, transform, supercharge, robust, seamless, paradigm, the future is here, in today's fast-paced world. NONE.
11. **Emojis.** None on LinkedIn body. (X is dormant; if it returns, at most one, only if it genuinely earns it, usually none.)
12. **Identical sentence lengths back to back.** Vary aggressively. Drop a 4-word sentence next to a 28-word one. Length variance is the strongest human signal.
13. **Hollow listicles** ("5 things X taught me about Y") with no specific substance under each.

### What humans actually do (lean in)
- **Mix sentence lengths hard.** Fragments are good. "Which was wrong." "I missed it." "Still chewing on this one."
- **Be specific in ways people believe but can't verify.** Not "recently" but "last Tuesday." Not "our agent" but "the sales agent that sent 20 cold emails before I'd had coffee." Specificity is the strongest signal a real person wrote this from real experience.
- **Numbers that aren't round.** 37, not 30. $4,200, not $4,000.
- **Hedge.** "I think." "Maybe." "I might be wrong about this." Constant confidence reads as AI.
- **Self-correct mid-thought.** "Actually no, that's not the real reason." Models polish to one clean take; humans show the seams.
- **One concrete real-world anchor** per post — a hiring manager rejecting a stack of CVs in nine minutes, a People lead re-running her shortlist, a recruiter's offhand line in a demo, a candidate's question you couldn't answer cleanly.
- **End open.** Real life doesn't resolve. "Ask me in two weeks." "I don't know what to do with this yet."

## LinkedIn voice — verdict first (ACTIVE channel)

LinkedIn is the live channel. These rules keep the feed out of news-anchor mode (headline, two stats, a "Catch:", a rhetorical question — polished, safe, and nobody's) and in the founder-with-a-position mode senior buyers actually reply to. They apply to every pillar.

1. **Open with the opinion, not the news.** Line 1 is what Ahmed thinks; the facts come after, as the evidence that earns it. The hook must stand alone above the "see more" fold and a reader should be able to *disagree* with it. If the first line could appear on a hiring-news roundup unchanged, it fails. Strongest opener types: contrarian myth-buster ("Most recruiters optimize the wrong number"), curiosity-gap state-then-withhold ("We let an AI run the first-round screen across a range of companies. The thing that broke wasn't the model."), named-number data-shock ("Only 26% of candidates trust AI to judge them fairly."), and confession / cost-of-being-wrong ("I built an AI interviewer and still got hiring wrong for 18 months.").
2. **Rotate the closer.** Before drafting, read the last 5 LinkedIn entries in `state/posting-log.md` and note how each ends. **At most 1 in 3 posts may end on a question.** The rest end on a verdict ("The resume is a fine introduction. It is a terrible judge."), an owned bet, or a dry one-liner. If the previous two LinkedIn posts both closed on questions, a question close is banned this run. When you do close on a question, make it specific and answerable ("What's the one screening signal you trust that an AI can't see yet?"), never a generic "thoughts?".
3. **One strong line per post, and let it close.** If you write a genuinely great sentence, END on it. Stapling a rhetorical question after your best line dilutes it.
4. **Banned tics** (they become house fingerprints): "Catch:", "Buried:", "One thing buried...", engagement bait ("comment YES if you agree", reaction polls — detected and throttled), and more than two consecutive stat fragments. Weave numbers into real sentences and keep only the one or two that carry the take. No links in the body (50-70% reach penalty); if a link is essential, it goes in the first comment.
5. **Builder anchor, regularly.** At least every third post connects the take to Ahmed's own lived vantage building AI interviewers ("I build these systems for a living, and the hard part was never making the model fast"). Generic and truthful. ⚠ Never internal Ployo specifics: no which-model-in-prod, no internal cost numbers, no client names. The anchor is a lived lens, not a data leak.
6. **Honor the messaging spine on sensitive ground.** On bias, surveillance, regulation, and "a robot rejected me," take the explainable / human-in-the-loop / bias-aware side. AI does not remove bias on its own; the credible founder line is that it can make assessment legible so bias becomes fixable, with a human still signing the hire. Cheerleading AI as the fix for broken hiring reads as naive or self-serving to this audience.

### Format mechanics (LinkedIn)
- **Default to text.** Text wins comments (the signal that matters with senior buyers); carousels win impressions. Skew to text. Reserve carousels for ~1x/week genuine frameworks, numbered teardowns, before/after cases, or data decks (see `carousel.md`).
- **~150-300 words / ~800-2,000 characters.** Long enough to force the "see more" click and develop one real idea, short enough for a phone.
- **One post = one idea.** Shape: hook (lines 1-2, stands alone above the fold), context/tension with a concrete detail or number (lines 3-8), the payoff/verdict (lines 9-15), then a single specific question OR a strong closing line.
- **Heavy whitespace**, 1-2 sentences per line, blank line between beats.
- **Reply to comments in the first 60-90 minutes.** This is part of the cadence, not optional — it keeps the thread alive during the window that decides reach.

## Exemplar posts — the target sound (LinkedIn)

Calibration only, never reuse verbatim. Note the shape: opinion-first opener that stands alone, a concrete hiring anchor, numbers woven into sentences, the verdict landing last. One exemplar per pillar.

**The state of hiring (verdict close):**
> Most resume screening optimizes for the wrong signal, and we keep doubling down on it anyway.
>
> Think about what a resume actually rewards. It rewards the person who knew which keywords to mirror, who had a friend in the loop, who could afford someone to format the thing. None of that is the work. It is a proxy for the work, and a weak one.
>
> Then we bolt an ATS on top and make the proxy worse. The system scans for "stakeholder management" and "cross-functional," so candidates learn to stuff those exact phrases into every bullet. Now you are not even reading a record of what someone did. You are reading their guess about what your filter wants to hear.
>
> I watched a hiring manager last quarter reject a stack of CVs in under nine minutes. Forty-some applicants. The ones that survived were not the strongest people. They were the best-formatted documents. He knew it too. He shrugged and said the good ones will apply again.
>
> That line stuck with me. We built a process so noisy that we treat losing real talent as acceptable shrinkage.
>
> The resume was never measuring capability. It measures access, polish, and pattern-matching to a job ad. Screen on that and you don't get the best candidate. You get the best applicant. Those are different people, and the gap between them is where most hiring goes quietly wrong.
>
> The resume is a fine introduction. It is a terrible judge.

**AI x hiring (regulation/bias, verdict close):**
> Most "AI hiring is biased" panic gets the threat backwards. The bias was already there, sitting in your hiring managers' gut calls, and nobody was writing any of it down.
>
> NYC's Local Law 144 and the EU AI Act do care about fairness. But the way they enforce it is harder than most teams expect: prove how a candidate was assessed, and make that proof survive an audit. A human who passes on someone because they "weren't a culture fit" leaves nothing behind to check. A model can be required to log every signal it weighed, which means it can be checked, argued with, and corrected.
>
> The catch is that this only holds if the tool was built to record its reasoning. Most AI screeners are black boxes too, which is the part the panic never mentions. You can't audit what was never recorded, human or machine, and that is the whole game now.
>
> So the bar I hold us to is plain. Every score comes with its reasons attached. A recruiter reads why a candidate advanced or didn't, and they can override it. The model proposes, a person decides, the disagreement gets logged too.
>
> Most teams are bracing for these laws like weather. The ones who get ahead will treat explainability as the product, not the paperwork.
>
> Show me the reasons, or it doesn't ship.

**Founder lens (verdict close):**
> Most "AI hiring" pitches I see lead with speed. Faster screening, faster scheduling, faster rejections. Speed was never the part recruiters were ashamed of.
>
> I learned this the slow way, selling to TA leaders.
>
> The first version of our pitch was all throughput. Hundreds of candidates screened in the time it used to take to read ten resumes. People nodded. Nobody bought. Then one head of talent said something I still think about: "I can already reject people quickly. What I can't do is explain why, two weeks later, when the candidate emails me asking."
>
> That reframed the whole problem. The pain in hiring at volume isn't the screening. It's the part nobody logs. The gut call at 4pm on a Friday that becomes a real decision about a real person's month.
>
> Speed makes that worse, not better. If your process is biased or vague or undocumented, going faster just produces more biased, vague, undocumented decisions. You scale the mess.
>
> What actually moves a TA leader is the opposite of magic. Show them the reasoning. Every score tied to something a human can read, push back on, and overrule. Less "trust the AI" and more "here is the receipt, and you are still the one signing it."
>
> We rebuilt the entire pitch around that. The demo barely mentions speed now.
>
> The recruiters who get the most out of AI are not the ones trying to hire faster. They are the ones finally able to say why they hired at all.

**The state of hiring (question close, the rationed kind):**
> Most teams think candidates ghost because they're flaky. They ghost because we trained them to.
>
> A person spends 40 minutes on an application, uploads a resume, then retypes the same resume into seven boxes. Silence for three weeks. A rejection that reads like it came from a vending machine, if it comes at all. We do this a few hundred times a month and act surprised that the good ones stop replying.
>
> Here's the part that stings. The candidates most likely to walk are the ones you most want. Strong people have options, so they have a lower tolerance for being left on read. Your worst process selects against your best hires. You're not filtering for talent. You're filtering for patience.
>
> I build AI interviewers, so people assume my answer is "automate the screening." It's narrower. The fix is closing the loop. Every applicant gets a real first conversation within a day. Every person hears back with something specific, even a no. Basic respect, done at a scale humans can't hit by hand.
>
> Most "candidate experience" budgets get spent on the careers page. Nice photos, a values statement, a video of the office dog. None of it matters once someone is inside the funnel and waiting.
>
> If your time-to-first-response is measured in weeks, what exactly is your careers page apologizing for?

## Hard never
- Never claim a Ployo capability/number that isn't true. Never invent benchmarks or studies. Never overclaim ("AI that's 10x more accurate"); specific-but-unverifiable lived detail lands, unbacked superlatives don't.
- **Never pose as a career recruiter.** The credible seat is builder-in-the-arena: "I build AI interviewers, and here's what I've seen," not "in my 15 years as a recruiter." Actual TA leaders will sniff out borrowed practitioner credentials instantly. Speak from what you've built and sold, never from a recruiting career you don't have.
- Never punch at a named person or @handle, and never dunk on a named competitor. Spicy about ideas and the system (ATS theater, interview theater), never about people.
- Never cheerlead AI as the fix for broken hiring; hold the explainable / human-in-the-loop / bias-aware line on sensitive ground.
- Never leak internal Ployo specifics: which model is in prod, internal costs, client names.
- Never post about a launch/event/number you couldn't verify against a real source this run (see `guardrails.md`).
